# Nobody else bundles the whole booth

The market app is marketable, but only as part of the bundle. No product on the US market in 2025-2026 does what Maker Market does as one package: a maker website and catalog, a phone checkout that treats Venmo, Cash App, Zelle and cash as real payment types with a "waiting to pay, then Paid or Not received" step, a scan-to-buy page that holds a one-of-a-kind piece so it can't also sell online, and a per-market record that shows whether a show was worth the booth fee. Every one of those pieces exists somewhere on its own. A rush of tiny new vendor apps launched between late 2025 and 2026, and some are cheap or free, so the market app cannot be sold as a standalone tracker or POS. Booth-tracking tools go for $0 to $15 a month, or $6.99 once. The strongest pitch is "your website, plus the booth tools nobody else has, for about the cost of one craft-fair booth a year." The median craft-fair booth costs **$165**, and Maker Market's yearly price is **$150**. The real risks are less about competitors and more about whether buyers will take the extra step at a busy table, how reliably Venmo and Cash App can be opened with the price already filled in, and the fact that makers still need Square or something like it for cards.

## Today's booth is Square, a Venmo sign and a spreadsheet

The typical US maker at a craft fair runs three tools that don't talk to each other. In every 2026 vendor guide found, Square is the default card reader for craft fairs and farmers markets ([VendorsMap](https://vendorsmap.com/blog/credit-card-readers-craft-fairs); [findhomegrown](https://findhomegrown.com/blog/best-payment-methods-for-farmers-market-vendors)). Next to it sits a printed "Scan to Pay" sign with a separate Venmo, Cash App and PayPal code. Those signs are common enough that people sell them on Etsy ([Etsy](https://www.etsy.com/market/craft_fair_payment)). Venmo is described as **one of the most common ways buyers pay at craft fairs** ([QuadraPay](https://quadrapay.com/accept-payments-vendor-fairs-craft-markets/)), and some organizers now push every vendor to take at least one electronic payment, as the 2025 NC State Crafts Fair did ([NCSU](https://crafts.arts.ncsu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/77/2025/04/2025-Crafts-Fair-Information-Sheet.pdf)). No one has measured the actual split between cash, cards and payment apps at US craft fairs, so this picture rests on guides and organizers, not a survey.

The payment apps are cheap for the maker. A Venmo business profile costs **1.9% + 10¢** per payment ([Venmo Help](https://help.venmo.com/hc/en-us/articles/1500003204302-Business-Profile-Fees-)). Cash App for Business takes **2.75%** ([Cash App Help](https://cash.app/help/6521-cash-for-business-fees)). Zelle has no fee through the bank ([Fit Small Business](https://fitsmallbusiness.com/how-to-use-zelle-for-business/)). The catch is that those codes only carry money. They carry no information about what was bought. The maker has to watch their phone, read a name or note, and remember which piece it was for. Nothing holds the item, and nothing adds the sale to that market's total. That is the gap the market app fills.

Square does go partway. It lets sellers create named payment types like "Venmo" or "Zelle" that show up separately in reports, though the feature is labeled beta ([Square Help](https://squareup.com/help/us/en/article/8611-capture-external-transactions-using-custom-payment-methods); [Human Extended](https://humanextended.com/how-to/add-custom-payment-method-to-square/)). Shopify's point of sale allows similar custom types, but sellers say its reports handle them badly, and some ring Venmo up as cash, which throws off the cash count ([Shopify Community](https://community.shopify.com/t/how-do-you-handle-mobile-payments-with-pos/72363)). Neither one has a "waiting to pay" state. They record the payment as received the moment the maker taps it. Neither has any idea of a "market" with dates, a booth fee, an organizer and a profit figure. Square sellers fake it by renaming their device for each event or creating a separate "location" per market ([Square Community](https://community.squareup.com/t5/Payments-Troubleshooting/Reports-for-separate-devices/td-p/691495); [Square Help](https://squareup.com/help/us/en/article/5580-manage-multiple-locations-with-square)). Then they subtract booth fees in a spreadsheet, which is what craft-fair guides tell them to do ([pandataps](https://pandataps.com/craft-pricing/guides/craft-fair-pricing-guide)).

## A dozen tiny apps chase the booth, and none bundles a website

The closest competitors are a wave of small, brand-new vendor apps, almost all launched between late 2025 and 2026, with one to three App Store ratings each. The need is real and recognized, but nobody has won yet. The table shows who covers which piece and at what price.

| Product | What it covers | Price | Missing vs. Maker Market |
|---|---|---|---|
| **BoothBrain** (iOS) | Phone checkout that records cash and opens Square, Venmo, Cash App and PayPal; sales by payment method; event prep lists; inventory ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/boothbrain/id6754667277)) | $9.99 or $14.99/mo | No website, no buyer scan page, no hold, no Paid/Not received step. 1 rating. |
| **VendStats** (iOS) | Quick sale logging, costs, profit, event-to-event comparison ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/vendstats-sales-tracker/id6759027702)) | $4.99/mo or $29.99/yr | No website, no buyer page. 3 ratings. |
| **BoothTally** (web) | Booth fees, sales and costs per fair, with a "Rebook / Watch fees / Skip" verdict ([BoothTally](https://boothtally.com/)) | $6.99 one time | Tracker only. Sales typed in by hand. |
| **Before The Booth** (web) | Show applications, deadlines, per-festival costs and return, mileage ([Before The Booth](https://www.beforethebooth.com/)) | Free; $12 or $18/mo | No checkout, no website. Looks very early. |

Two of these deserve a closer look. **BoothBrain** works almost exactly the way the Maker Market checkout works. It records cash and payment-app sales without handling the money, and it reports revenue by payment method. It charges **$9.99 to $14.99 a month for the checkout alone**, with no website ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/boothbrain/id6754667277)). Next to that, $15 for a website, catalog, checkout and market records is good value. **TallyTill** is the most direct competitor to the scan-to-buy half. It runs on the seller's own phone and sells at the same $15 price point. But it is built around scanning the item in front of you, which suits jars of honey, not "browse this market's pieces and hold the one I want." Its own site wasn't checked, so whether it accepts Venmo or connects to an online store is still unknown. It is the one competitor worth a hands-on look.

The only product that combines a website and a booth checkout is **Marketspread**. It is built for the people who run markets, and its store takes **2.5% of every order** or costs **$129 a month** ([Marketspread](https://marketspread.com/online-sales-for-small-vendors)). The other combined option is a real Shopify store, at about $29 to $39 a month, with its free in-person selling add-on ([NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/article/small-business/shopify-pricing); [startupowl](https://startupowl.com/reviews/shopify-pos)). That gives one shared inventory but none of the market-day tools.

## Three things no competitor has

The research found three features that don't exist anywhere else. The first is the **waiting-to-pay queue**. A buyer's name appears on the maker's phone, and the maker marks it Paid or Not received once the Venmo or Zelle payment actually shows up. No checkout app found has a "pending" state for payment-app money. They all assume the money arrived when the button was tapped. That queue also solves the obvious way the hold could be abused, where someone holds a piece and walks off. No competitor needs this, because none of them holds items.

The second is the **hold that pulls the piece from online sale**. Square and Shopify only reduce stock once a sale is finished, not when a buyer says "I want this one." The cross-platform workaround of syncing Etsy with Square has years of complaints about broken sync behind it ([Square Community](https://community.squareup.com/t5/Online-Store/Trouble-syncing-Etsy-and-Square/m-p/301697); [Etsy Community](https://community.etsy.com/t5/Technical-Issues/Etsy-and-Square-not-syncing/m-p/147274801)). The nearest thing to a hold is a Stripe payment link capped at one purchase ([Stripe](https://docs.stripe.com/no-code/payment-links)), but that means one QR code per piece, cards only. Square's self-serve QR ordering is built for restaurants. A Square community helper said retail sellers have to "get a little creative" ([Square Community](https://community.squareup.com/t5/Online-Store/Is-it-possible-to-use-Self-serve-ordering-in-retail/m-p/737422)). Square's self-serve kiosk doesn't support Venmo ([Square Seller Community](https://sellercommunity.com/t5/Square-Point-of-Sale/Square-Kiosk-for-Farm-Stands-Can-It-Support-Cash-Coupons-and/m-p/768257)).

The third is a **per-market record that fills itself in**. Makers clearly think in exactly these terms. The Etsy templates and free trackers ask for the same fields: booth fee, other costs, hours, notes, unsold stock, best fair ([Etsy listing](https://www.etsy.com/listing/4530231022/craft-fair-tracker-spreadsheet-craft); [Made Urban](https://www.madeurban.com/blog/7-stats-to-track-at-craft-shows-to-improve-sales/)). Vendors on the Etsy forums argue over whether a $275 booth was worth it, or describe spending $500 in fees across a season and earning about half back ([Etsy Community](https://community.etsy.com/t5/Craft-Fairs-it-s-a-living/Help-with-perspective-please/td-p/37741571); [Etsy Community](https://community.etsy.com/t5/Craft-Fairs-it-s-a-living/Why-can-t-I-let-it-go/td-p/102079297)). Every existing tracker needs the sales typed in afterward. In Maker Market, the booth sales already land in the market record, split by payment method. That is the honest difference from a $6.99 spreadsheet.

## $150 a year sits right at the price of one booth

The audience is large, but price-sensitive. One fair directory lists **30,509 craft fairs for 2026**, with November the busiest month at **5,081** ([TheCraftMap](https://www.thecraftmap.com/fairs/2026)). There are roughly **7,000 farmers markets** on top of that ([DataHub](https://datahub.io/harvestlymarkets/us-farmers-markets-dataset)). A September 2026 snapshot of nearly 9,600 events puts the **median craft-fair booth at $165** and the median farmers-market booth at **$30**, with a typical $30 application fee on top ([Canopy Season](https://canopyseason.com/blog/what-craft-fair-booth-fees-actually-cost/)). Vendor blogs estimate an average day at **$300 to $800** in sales ([VendorsMap](https://vendorsmap.com/blog/how-much-money-craft-fair)). Part-timers do one or two shows a month ([GetVendorLoop](https://getvendorloop.com/guides/how-craft-vendors-make-money)), and committed sellers on the Etsy forums report around 17 to 20 a year ([Etsy Community](https://community.etsy.com/t5/Craft-Fairs-it-s-a-living/How-many-shows-do-you-do-each-year/td-p/120177641)). All of these figures come from directories and blogs. None is a census.

Etsy's seller survey is the best stand-in for who these makers are. About **80% are women**, the average age is **43**, **89% work alone**, and the craft business brings in about **11% of household income** ([Patreon summary](https://www.patreon.com/posts/125639678); [LinkMyBooks](https://linkmybooks.com/blog/how-many-sellers-are-on-etsy)). That points to people who use phones comfortably but won't tolerate fiddly setup, and who watch every recurring charge. Building each site by hand for them is a real advantage with this group.

Against that, the pricing holds up. **$150 a year for Maker Market is less than one median craft-fair booth**, so the pitch writes itself: if it helps you drop one bad show, or saves one sale you'd have lost, it has paid for itself. It costs about the same as BoothBrain's checkout alone or The Craft Map's tracker alone, and both come without a website. **Maker Full at $25 a month ($250 a year)** sits in the same range as a basic Shopify store. That store has no market tools and usually needs paid add-ons that run $20 to $100 a month each ([GetOneCart](https://www.getonecart.com/shopify-fees/)). Where Maker Full wins is simplicity and the booth tools, not a lower price. Seasons matter here too. Fairs pile up in the fall and the holidays, and the first three months of the year are quiet. Monthly payers are likely to cancel in January. Pushing the yearly plan before the fall season is the natural fix.

## Where it could stumble

The biggest unknown is the buyer. People already scan a Venmo code at a booth without thinking twice. The scan-to-buy page adds a step before that: pick the piece and type your first name. No evidence was found either way on whether buyers will do that at a crowded table instead of just handing over cash. One Square seller who sells maple syrup refused to use per-product codes because many of his older customers found it "one tech step too far" ([Square Community](https://community.squareup.com/t5/Orders-Menu-Items-Catalog/Non-Restaurant-Self-Service-with-a-Handheld-Scanner/td-p/713344)). The flow probably earns its keep on busy days, when the maker is tied up, and on pricier one-of-a-kind pieces where the hold matters. It is likely overkill for a $5 item. The pitch should be framed that way.

The second risk is about opening payment apps with the price filled in. Venmo has an old link format that fills in the amount and a note, but Venmo has retired its developer tools, so whether that link still works today needs testing on real phones ([Venmo payment links](https://venmo.com/paymentlinks)). Cash App's payment links are made one at a time inside the app, and no web address format for filling in an amount was found ([Cash App press](https://cash.app/press/cash-app-launches-payment-links)). Zelle's code appears to identify only who gets paid, not how much ([Fulton Bank](https://www.fultonbank.com/-/media/Files/Personal/Zelle_Small-Business_FAQs.pdf)). If the price can't be filled in, the buyer has to type it, and wrong amounts become a real problem. The Paid or Not received step then matters even more.

Third, cards aren't going away. Guides claim roughly 70% of 2026 craft-fair purchases are card or phone tap, though that figure isn't traced to a study ([VendorsMap](https://vendorsmap.com/blog/credit-card-readers-craft-fairs)). Makers will keep Square or a tap-to-pay app beside Maker Market. So Maker Market has to make it painless to record a card sale taken on Square, or the market totals will have holes in them. It also can't beat the card processors on fees, which all land around $1.05 to $1.20 on a $40 sale. Square's offline card mode, with its 24-hour sessions and the seller carrying the risk of declines ([Square Help](https://squareup.com/help/us/en/article/5095-process-offline-payments-with-square)), is the benchmark for weak signal at outdoor events. Spotty cell service is the most documented booth complaint ([Kolton Consulting](https://kolton.consulting/resources/card-reader-loses-signal-farmers-market)).

Fourth, the competition is cheap and crowded, even if it is weak. Per-show tracking on its own is free or under $10 one time ([BoothTally](https://boothtally.com/); [BoothKeeper](https://www.boothkeeper.com/)), which sets a low price anchor. Several solo developers built nearly the same tracker within months of each other. Building one is easy. Reaching makers is the hard part. A tax angle won't help much either: the 2025 federal law put the 1099-K reporting threshold back at $20,000 and 200 transactions, so most hobby sellers fall under it again ([Avalara](https://www.avalara.com/us/en/blog/one-big-beautiful-bill-act-1099-reporting-threshold.html)). One related gap is real, though. Sales tax rules and rates follow the event's location and vary state by state ([Avalara state guide](https://www.avalara.com/us/en/learn/whitepapers/craft-fairs-and-sales-tax-a-state-by-state-guide.html)). Makers who cross state lines would value a tax rate set per market.

There are also limits in the evidence itself. No Reddit discussion could be read, so firsthand vendor complaints about payment-app mix-ups, screenshot fraud, or mismatched names are missing. There is no measured payment split for craft fairs, and no download or user numbers for any of the vendor apps.

## Conclusion

The research changes where the selling point sits. The market app isn't really competing with Square. It competes with the habit of using Square, a Venmo sign and a notebook, and the thing that habit can't do is connect a Venmo payment to a specific piece and a specific market. That connection is what to sell: "every sale at the show, however they paid, lands in that show's tally, and the piece comes off your website the moment someone claims it." The website gives makers a reason to keep paying all year. The market tools are what no one else has. Craft-fair makers, who pay around $165 a booth and sell one-of-a-kind pieces they also list online, have far more reason to buy than farmers-market vendors, who pay $30 a week and sell jars of jam.

The practical next steps before leaning on the pitch are mostly checks, not features. Test on real phones whether Venmo and Cash App will open with the price already filled in. Look hands-on at TallyTill, the one competitor at the same price on the self-checkout side. Watch a few real buyers at Rose n' Cat's table to see whether they'll pick a piece and type their name, or just reach for cash. Those three answers will show whether the scan-to-buy page is the headline feature or a nice extra behind the market records and the Paid or Not received step.
