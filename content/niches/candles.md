# Candles

## What this business does

A candle maker pours and sells candles. Most are small operations — one or two people working out of a kitchen, a converted garage, a rented studio, or a corner of a shared workshop. The work involves sourcing wax, fragrance oils, wicks, and vessels and combining them into finished candles. Distribution ranges across in-person sales at craft fairs and farmers markets, third-party marketplaces (Etsy is the dominant one), wholesale into small retail shops, and direct-to-consumer sales through the maker's own site.

The category is wide. It includes candles framed as self-care, candles framed as decor, candles tied to religious or devotional practice, candles tied to ritual or spiritual practice, candles made primarily as gifts, candles made for specific subcultures, and candles made as part of a broader home-fragrance line. The maker's positioning shapes nearly everything downstream — voice, price, vessel, distribution, photography, customer.

## Brand exemplars across the range

These are real brands cited not as a target the maker should imitate but as anchors showing what a polished version of different positionings looks like in this category. The AI should treat them as the high-water mark of what a million-dollar brand achieves in a given direction, and use them to ground how a tenant's storefront could feel if they sit in a similar direction.

**P.F. Candle Co. (California artisanal, accessible-premium).** Earthy, warm, heritage-led. "Poured in California," scent families as primary navigation, descriptive note-pair names (Teakwood & Tobacco, Amber & Moss). Voice is casual and grounded.

**Harlem Candle Co. (luxury storytelling, cultural).** Rich jewel tones, gold accents, candles named for cultural figures (Eartha, after Eartha Kitt). Tagline: "Every Scent Tells a Story." Story-led homepage, press credibility, premium pricing.

**Boy Smells (design-forward, playful, queer-coded).** Bold colors, named-as-character scents (Cowboy Kush, Vanilla Era, Woodphoria), conversational copy, ingredient lists rather than perfumery notes. Distinctive voice that customers buy into as much as the product.

**Brooklyn Candle Studio (minimalist artisanal, destination collections).** Clean photography on neutral backgrounds, scent families plus place-based collections (Italian Coast, Catskills, Santorini), strong "vegan, cruelty-free, phthalate-free, petroleum-free" positioning.

**Otherland (design-forward, art-inspired).** "Perfumer crafted. Art inspired." Sensory profile organization, premium D2C positioning, design pieces as much as candles.

**Devotional and religious candle makers (functional, traditional, faith-led).** Iconography on the vessel, traditional palettes from the tradition, plainer typography, often paraffin or beeswax, lower price points. Different visual rules entirely. A different customer entirely.

**Folk and vintage Etsy makers (handcrafted-personal, story-led, lower-priced).** Warm earth tones, hand-lettered or vintage typography, often single-maker operations, often selling at $12–$20 per candle, often leaning into a personal story or local angle. The voice is conversational and the production is visibly small.

**Occult and ritual candle makers (dark, apothecary-coded).** Deep palettes (black, oxblood, midnight green), gothic or apothecary typography, candlelight photography, intention-based scent names. Often paraffin or beeswax, often with herbs or crystals embedded.

A tenant could land anywhere across this range, including places not on this list. The mood pick and the tenant's own inspiration URLs at onboarding tell the AI which direction to lean.

## Who their customers are

Buyer types in this category vary widely. The most common ones include:

**Self-buyers** treating themselves to a small indulgence or trying to make a room feel like a different place.

**Gift-buyers** picking something for holidays, housewarmings, weddings, birthdays, thank-yous, and "I was thinking of you" gestures. Gift-buyers are often less confident than self-buyers and lean on bundles, best-sellers, and the maker's own recommendations.

**Functional buyers** picking candles for a specific use — power outages, meditation, religious observance, scent for a specific room.

**Collectors and followers** who track specific makers and buy across their line. Often subscription customers.

**Subcultural buyers** for whom the candle is partly an identity signal — queer-coded, witchy, ritual-practicing, regional-pride, scene-specific.

Across most segments, two concerns recur:

**Scent throw.** Hot throw is how strongly the candle scents the room when lit; cold throw is what it smells like unlit. "Weak throw" or "smells nothing when lit" is the most common negative review across the category at every price point. Buyers learn this vocabulary quickly.

**What's in it.** Wax type (soy, coconut, beeswax, paraffin, blends), fragrance source (essential oils, fragrance oils, phthalate status), wick material (cotton, wood, hemp), and what's deliberately not in it (paraben-free, cruelty-free, vegan claims are common). The weight buyers put on ingredients varies by segment.

Price sensitivity varies by positioning. Across the category, common price points fall roughly into these bands, with overlap and exception: small candles (2–4 oz) at $8–$22, standard single-wick (8–10 oz) at $14–$36, larger 2-wick or 3-wick (11–26 oz) at $28–$80. A maker at the lower end of a band positions differently than a maker at the upper end. The same physical candle costs different amounts in different brand directions.

## How they talk about their products

The dominant vocabulary in this category comes from perfumery. Scent gets broken into top notes (the first impression — often citrus, herbal, light), middle or heart notes (florals, fruits, spices), and base notes (woody, vanilla, musk, resinous, long-lasting). A candle's scent description in this category often follows a three-part shape: "top notes of A and B, a heart of C and D, settling into E and F."

Naming strategies vary widely across the brand exemplars above. Some examples of approaches in the market:

**Descriptive note-pairs** that signal the scent profile directly — Teakwood & Tobacco, Amber & Moss, Ojai Lavender. The name tells the customer what they'll smell.

**Named-as-character** where the name does personality work and the description does the scent work — Cowboy Kush, Vanilla Era, Woodphoria.

**Place names** evoking a setting — Italian Coast, Catskills, Santorini, Golden Coast.

**Cultural references** drawn from history, music, art, or community — Eartha (after Eartha Kitt).

**Intention or function names** common in ritual, devotional, and occult positions — "Cleansing," "Protection," specific saints' names, "Money Drawing."

**Time-of-day, seasonal, or mood names** — "Morning Set," "Autumn Hearth," "Slow Sunday."

No single naming strategy is the right one. The strategy follows the positioning.

What recurs across positionings is sensory and grounded language. Verbs like "pour" and "blend." Phrases like "hand-poured," "small-batch," specific burn-time claims, specific ingredient claims. Corporate phrasing — "premium quality," "luxury experience," "the finest" — reads flat across the category regardless of price tier, because handcraft positioning and corporate phrasing fight each other.

Story carries weight in this category. Buyers often want to know who made the candle, why they started, what they were trying to capture. A storefront with a visible maker tends to outperform one without.

## Common specializations and variations

These are variation axes that exist in the candle market. The seller defines their own variations per product — these are starting suggestions, not a fixed schema.

**Scent.** The core dimension. The category broadly organizes scents into families: Woody, Floral, Citrus or Fresh, Fruity, Gourmand, Spicy, Green or Herbal, Aquatic. Family is one common navigation axis; collection (seasonal, themed, place-based, intention-based) is another; mood is another. Many makers use more than one organization at once.

**Size.** Travel and sample sizes commonly sit at 2–4 oz. Standard single-wick sizes commonly sit at 8–10 oz. Larger 2-wick and 3-wick candles commonly sit at 11–26 oz. The category also includes pillar candles, tea lights, votives, wax melts, tapers, and devotional/novena candles in glass cylinders.

**Wax type.** Common options include soy, coconut, beeswax, paraffin, palm, and blends. Each carries different positioning, burn characteristics, and price implications. Soy is common in clean-ingredient positioning; coconut blends often sit at a premium step above soy; beeswax appears in natural-and-traditional positioning; paraffin appears across the category, especially in devotional, religious, and lower-priced segments.

**Wick.** Cotton is common. Wood wicks crackle when burning and are specifically sought by some buyers. Hemp appears occasionally. Wick count itself is a variation — single-wick is most common; 2-wick and 3-wick exist mostly in larger vessels.

**Vessel.** Glass jars (clear, frosted, colored, embossed) are common. Metal tins are common, especially for travel sizes and lower price points. Ceramic vessels are common in higher-positioned lines and at the artisanal end; the reusability angle (planter, trinket dish, decor) is a real selling point worth surfacing where it applies. Vintage or repurposed vessels appear in some maker positions. Open pillars without a vessel exist as their own segment. Devotional candles often use tall glass cylinders with iconography printed on them.

**Burn time.** Usually expressed in hours or a range. Buyers compare across products; inflated claims breed negative reviews.

Bundle and collection patterns common in this category: trios of related scents, seasonal sets, candle plus accessory bundles (wick trimmer, matches, snuffer), themed collections, place-based collections, intention-based collections, and limited-edition drops. Subscription models also appear, with "scent of the month" being a recurring format.

## What customers ask before buying

The AI's job in product descriptions, FAQ, and storefront copy is to answer these without the customer having to ask:

- How long does it burn? (Specific, not inflated.)
- How strong is the scent — both lit (hot throw) and unlit (cold throw)?
- What's in it? Wax type, fragrance source, anything to avoid for sensitivities, anything to highlight that the maker cares about.
- Is it safe around pets or kids? (Pet-safety is a recurring concern for some scents, particularly essential-oil-based ones.)
- What does it look like lit and unlit?
- How is it shipped? Will it arrive intact? Cracked vessels on arrival is a recurring complaint in the category.
- Is the vessel reusable, and what for?

A storefront that answers these clearly outperforms one that doesn't, regardless of design direction.

## Visual direction range

The candle category contains a wide range of visual sensibilities. The mood pick at onboarding and the tenant's inspiration URLs are what choose among them.

Directions observed across the brand exemplars above, plus the broader market:

- **Warm artisanal:** earthy palettes, serif type, generous whitespace, lifestyle photography emphasizing texture and natural light. Common in candles framed as self-care.
- **Minimal modern:** monochrome or limited palette, sans-serif type, clean product photography on solid backgrounds.
- **Luxe ornate:** rich jewel tones, metallic accents, ornamental type, ceremonial-feeling photography. Common in luxury and storytelling positions.
- **Design-forward playful:** bold palettes, distinctive type, named-as-character branding. Common in queer-coded, scene-coded, or art-coded positions.
- **Dark, occult, apothecary:** deep palettes, gothic or apothecary type, candlelight photography. Common in ritual and intention positions.
- **Folk, vintage, traditional:** muted earth tones, traditional or hand-lettered type, period-evoking photography. Common in Etsy-native single-maker shops.
- **Subcultural or scene-specific:** palettes and type drawn from the maker's scene. Common where the maker is selling to a specific community.
- **Devotional / religious / functional:** traditional iconography, plainer typography, palettes specific to the tradition. Common in saint, novena, and ritual candles.

A given storefront might combine elements across directions. The mood pick narrows it; inspiration URLs sharpen it further.

What stays true across directions: candle photography rewards close-ups. The texture of the wax pour, the flame at full burn, the light catching the vessel, the wick mid-crackle — these are the shots that do visual work in the category. The AI should encourage close-up product photography across whatever aesthetic direction the tenant has chosen.

## What tends to surface on the storefront

Blocks and widgets the AI should weigh when composing a candle maker's storefront. The mood pick and inspiration URLs drive the layout; this just says what tends to be useful in the category.

**A scent-organized grid or filter.** Scent is the primary variation. Whatever the visual direction, customers benefit from being able to see scents grouped or filtered by family, mood, or collection.

**A collections section.** Seasonal, themed, place-based, intention-based, or mood-based collections give the AI a way to organize a line into coherent groups instead of a flat wall.

**A best-sellers or featured-products section.** Candles are hard to choose blind, especially for gift-buyers.

**A story-led about section.** The maker's story is part of the product in this category, across positionings.

**A featured product treatment.** A single candle photographed beautifully tends to do more storefront work than the same candle thumbnailed in a grid.

**A gift-finder or curated bundles section.** A meaningful share of candle purchases are gifts. Sets and curated picks help gift-buyers decide.

**A repeat-purchase nudge.** Candles are consumable. Subscriptions, favorites pulls, and "if you liked X, try Y" suggestions all play well.

**A sustainability or ingredients section or block.** What's in the candle (and what isn't) is decisive for a meaningful share of buyers in many segments. Where the maker has committed to specific ingredient practices, surfacing them clearly tends to help.

## What to avoid

- **Stock-photo candle imagery.** The category is built on handcraft across every positioning. Stock imagery undercuts that regardless of aesthetic direction.
- **Vague generic descriptions.** "A relaxing scent for unwinding" is dead copy. Specific scent language ("bergamot, vetiver, and a thread of black pepper") works across the category.
- **Inflated burn times.** Buyers compare. Negative reviews follow.
- **Marketing claims without context.** "100% soy wax" is meaningful to buyers who know why; copy should briefly explain why when soy or coconut or beeswax is the selling point. "Clean-burning" without saying what makes it clean is empty.
- **Treating the candle like a deliverable instead of an experience.** "We sell candles" reads flatter than "here's the morning I built this scent around" across the category.
- **Burying the maker.** Buyers in this category want to know there's a person behind the candle. Storefronts that hide the maker tend to underperform.

## Adjacent niches

Candle makers commonly expand into soap, bath products, room sprays, reed diffusers, room and linen mists, and other small home-fragrance items. Several of the brand exemplars above (P.F. Candle Co., Brooklyn Candle Studio, Boy Smells) have done exactly this. A maker who starts with candles and adds soap should be encouraged to set up a second collection rather than mix categories in one grid. The niche files for soap, bath and body, and home fragrance share much of the voice and customer profile in this file; when those files get written, the authors should lean on this one for the shared parts and focus their files on what's specific to those products (curing time and ingredient transparency for soap, diffuser oil and stick care for diffusers, and so on).
