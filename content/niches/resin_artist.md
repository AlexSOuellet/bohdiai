# Resin Artist

## What this business does

A resin artist casts, coats, and cures liquid resin into finished objects — pouring pigmented resin into silicone molds, doming it over surfaces, or flooding it across wood and canvas, then letting it harden into a clear, glossy solid. The range of what gets made is wide: coasters, trays, and cheese or charcuterie boards; jewelry like earrings, pendants, and rings; wall art and ocean art; geode-style pieces; river tables and other furniture; keepsake and memorial pieces that preserve flowers, ashes, or hair; petri dishes, bookmarks, and trinket or ring dishes. Independent makers range from solo crafters working at a kitchen bench with a handful of molds, to studio artists producing large fluid-art canvases, to furniture shops flattening and pouring hardwood slabs into commissioned dining tables.

The work turns on the chemistry. Two-part epoxy resin is mixed by ratio, degassed of bubbles, tinted with pigments, micas, or alcohol inks, and left to cure over hours or days; UV resin cures in minutes under a lamp and suits small, thin pieces. Makers cast into silicone molds, dome over a flat blank, or do deep pours for thick blocks and river channels. Techniques carry their own names — lacing and cells for ocean waves, dirty pours and swirls for abstract art, embedding for flowers and objects. Because resin captures whatever is set into it, one-of-a-kind variation is the norm rather than the exception, and no two pours land identically.

Distribution channels include the maker's own online shop, Etsy, craft fairs and markets, wholesale to boutiques and gift shops, and commissioned custom work. Many resin artists run a ready-to-ship line of small goods alongside a made-to-order service — preservation keepsakes and river tables in particular are almost entirely commission-based, often reserved with a deposit before the customer ships in their materials.

## Brand exemplars across the range

**HalfBakedArt (Jane Biven).** A resin artist in Roswell, Georgia working in epoxy since 2012, with thousands of pours behind her and a studio-gallery, workshop, and commission practice. Fine-art fluid abstracts are the signature — original paintings, giclée prints, and licensing — alongside a side business retailing EcoPoxy and teaching technique and safety. Positioned around the artist's own hand and expertise.

**Chagrin Valley Custom Furniture.** A Cleveland-area shop building live-edge epoxy river tables and hardwood furniture, priced from around $1,900 into the five figures. Every piece is commissioned from the customer's own design, wood slab, and river color. "Extraordinary river tables — informed by nature and inspired by you." The positioning is high-craft, made-in-USA custom furniture, not a product line.

**Fossil Flowers.** A preservation studio turning wedding bouquets and memorial flowers into resin blocks, trays, ring holders, and keepsakes ($70–$1,100). Work is reserved with a deposit, the customer ships in their blooms, and every piece is dried, arranged, and poured to order. The positioning is sentimental and story-first — "created to honor your story."

**Flowers Forever & Bellabeads.** A 30-year flower-preservation and memorial-jewelry company in Columbia, South Carolina, with a staffed team of jewelers. Preserved-flower and cremation jewelry — pendants, rings, charm beads, cufflinks — made from a customer's wedding or funeral flowers, ashes, or a pet's fur ($70–$190). The authority story is longevity and a physical studio you can visit.

**Ocean and coastal resin decor makers.** Studios like Serene Life Art and the Resin Society build ocean and beach wall art — epoxy waves, sand, and shells worked into three-dimensional coastal scenes. The positioning is decor for a specific aesthetic: buyers furnishing a coastal or nature-themed room.

**Botanical resin jewelry makers.** Etsy-native shops preserving real pressed flowers, ferns, and mushrooms inside bezel-set pendants and earrings, many founded by makers who pivoted into resin during 2020. Nature-forward, gift-priced, and high-volume — the entry point for a lot of new resin sellers building a product line.

A tenant could land anywhere across this range, including places not on this list — a maker selling only food-safe cheese boards, a jeweler working exclusively in UV resin, a functional-homeware line of trays and trinket dishes, or an abstract painter who never casts a mold.

## Who their customers are

Buyers in this category tend to sort by what they're buying resin *for*.

**Self-buyers and home decorators** furnishing a room — an ocean canvas, a geode wall piece, a set of coasters, a trinket dish. They respond to the one-of-a-kind nature of the work and to how a piece will read in a specific space.

**Gift buyers** choosing something handmade for a birthday, wedding, housewarming, or holiday. Resin jewelry and small homeware are common gifts. This segment values personalization and clear guidance on what makes a good gift.

**Functional buyers** who want the object to work — a cheese board that's safe for food, a coaster that won't mar under a hot mug, a tray that holds up to daily use. These buyers ask pointed questions about food safety, heat, and water resistance before they buy.

**Sentiment and keepsake clients** commissioning preservation work — a wedding bouquet cast into a block, memorial flowers or ashes set into jewelry, a pet's fur in a pendant. This is the most emotionally weighted segment; trust, communication, and care with irreplaceable materials close the sale, and the client is usually willing to wait and pay more.

**Collectors and art buyers** buying fluid-art canvases or sculptural geode pieces as art rather than function. They read the artist's statement and want the maker's hand and story.

Recurring concerns cut across most segments: whether a piece is food-safe and heat-resistant, whether it will yellow or stay clear over time, how to care for and clean it, how much it weighs, whether resin is toxic once cured, and how closely a finished custom piece will match the photos given that every pour is unique. Price ranges vary widely by product and positioning. Earrings and small pendants run roughly $15–$60; coasters and trinket dishes $20–$70; trays and cheese boards $40–$150; larger wall art and ocean pieces $150–$800+; preservation keepsakes $70–$1,100; and river tables and furniture from around $1,900 into the five figures. Positioning shifts all of these.

## How they talk about their products

The vocabulary of this category is split between the chemistry and the effect. On the materials side, copy names the resin type and behavior: epoxy resin (two-part, mixed by ratio, thicker pours), UV resin (cures under a lamp, thin layers), casting resin and deep-pour resin (for thick blocks and river channels), doming resin (self-leveling coat that domes over an edge). Makers reference pot life and cure time, degassing bubbles, and food-safe or FDA-compliant when a piece touches food.

On the color and technique side, the language is about what the resin was made to do: pigments, micas, and alcohol inks for color; cells and lacing for the honeycomb and wave effects in ocean art; dirty pours and swirls for abstracts; embedding or encapsulating for flowers and objects; crushed glass or druzy for geode edges. Jewelry copy leans on bezels, the metal setting the resin sits in, and on preserved or pressed botanicals — real ferns, roses, forget-me-nots, hydrangea petals.

Naming strategies span the range. Descriptive names state the object and finish ("ocean wave charcuterie board," "clear resin heart"). Character or intention names carry the sentiment ("Timeless Blossom Pendant," "Circle of Memories Ring," "Crown of Memories"). Place and nature references anchor coastal and botanical work. Preservation makers lean hardest on story — the flower it came from, the person it honors. Flat copy tends to describe resin as just "shiny" or "pretty" without naming the technique, the material, or the story; the pieces that read as alive name the pour, the pigment, or the moment being preserved.

## Common specializations and variations

**Product category.** Coasters, trays, cheese and charcuterie boards, jewelry (earrings, pendants, rings), wall art and ocean art, geode art, river tables and furniture, keepsake and memorial pieces, petri dishes, bookmarks, trinket and ring dishes.

**Resin type.** Epoxy resin, UV resin, casting or deep-pour resin, doming resin.

**Technique.** Casting into silicone molds, doming, deep pours, fluid-art pours (dirty pour, swirl), ocean lacing and cells, embedding and encapsulating objects.

**Color and finish.** Pigments, micas, alcohol inks, translucent tints, opaque pours, glitter and crushed glass, gold-leaf accents, matte versus high-gloss finish.

**Embedded materials.** Preserved and pressed flowers, dried botanicals, seashells and sand, wood, photos, ashes, hair, keepsake objects.

**Food-safe status.** Decorative only versus food-safe and heat-resistant for boards, trays, and coasters.

**Custom and commission service.** Made-to-order with the customer's colors, dimensions, or supplied materials; deposit-reserved preservation work; collaborative design for furniture.

## What customers ask before buying

The AI's job in product descriptions, FAQ, and storefront copy is to answer these without the customer having to ask:

- Is this food-safe and heat-resistant — can I put a hot mug or food on it?
- Will the resin yellow or stay clear over time, and how does it hold up in sunlight?
- Is resin toxic, and is it safe once fully cured?
- How do I clean and care for it — can it go in the dishwasher?
- How heavy is it?
- Since every pour is one-of-a-kind, how closely will mine match the photo?
- For custom or preservation work, how do I send my flowers or materials, and what's the process and turnaround?
- Do you take commissions, and what colors, sizes, or personalization can I choose?

## Adjacent niches

Resin artists commonly expand into jewelry making (bezel-set and mixed-media pieces), woodworking (the wood half of river tables, cheese boards, and live-edge pieces), and flower preservation and floristry (pressed and preserved botanical work overlaps heavily with the keepsake segment). Some cross into candle and home-fragrance goods or broader home decor as their product line grows. The resin artist's material vocabulary — resin types, pigments and micas, casting and doming, cure and food-safe caveats — carries directly into these adjacent categories.
