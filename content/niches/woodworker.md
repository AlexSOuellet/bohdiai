# Woodworker

## What this business does

A woodworker takes lumber — boards, slabs, turning blanks, salvage stock — and produces objects from it. The range is enormous: dining tables and chairs, dressers and sideboards, beds and benches, kitchen islands and built-in cabinetry, cutting boards and bowls and spoons, turned vessels and platters, mantels and picture frames, jewelry boxes and humidors, candle holders and trays, wooden signs and monograms, instruments, skateboards, surfboards, doors, windows. A single shop usually picks two or three of these to live in; the catalog of what a working woodworker can make is far wider than what any one shop offers.

Operations vary by what the shop makes. A turner with a lathe and a few gouges works very differently from a cabinetmaker with a slider saw, a thickness sander, and a CNC. A custom-commission furniture maker spends weeks on one piece, with most of the work invisible in the joinery; a personalized-gift Etsy shop runs a laser engraver and ships dozens of boards a week with the buyer's last name. Both are woodworkers. Some shops are one person at the bench; some are five-person crews handling design, joinery, finishing, and install. The work spans new construction (a piece built from scratch), built-ins (a piece scribed and installed in a specific home), repair (re-gluing a broken Windsor chair), and restoration (refinishing a 1920s dresser to its original shellac).

Distribution channels include a maker's own online shop, Etsy, craft fairs, the artisan side of trade shows, gallery representation for art-furniture and turned vessels, regional design boutiques, wholesale to home stores, and direct commission work for designers, architects, and homeowners. Some woodworkers also teach — weekend classes, full-week intensives, or YouTube channels that fund the shop.

## Brand exemplars across the range

**Thos. Moser.** Maine-based, founded in 1972 by Tom Moser, a former college English professor. Solid American cherry, walnut, ash. Shaker-influenced silhouettes built with mortise-and-tenon and exposed wedged joinery. Every piece signed and dated by its maker. Lifetime guarantee, generational positioning ("furniture meant to outlast you"). Mid-to-high pricing on chairs ($800–$2,500) and tables ($3,500–$12,000+). The reference point for American heritage-craftsman positioning in furniture.

**Sawkille Co.** Rhinebeck, New York. Design-led contemporary studio run by a husband-and-wife team. Hand-shaped Windsor-derived chairs, slab tables, low stools and benches in walnut and white oak. Visually severe restraint paired with a deeply traditional construction method. Sold through design galleries and a small direct line. Higher pricing tier, with chairs in the $1,800–$3,500 range and tables landing well above $10,000. Proof that contemporary American woodwork can hold its own at the gallery tier.

**Lostine.** Philadelphia-based. American-made design-led modern home goods: pegboards, side tables, candle holders, plant stands, shelving, small mirrors. Built around a clear contemporary aesthetic, smaller objects rather than full furniture lines, mid-tier pricing ($45–$650). Sells through their own site, design boutiques, and a wholesale catalog. Shows the small-batch modern-design positioning that sits between full-furniture studios and gift-makers.

**Andrew Pearce Bowls.** Vermont. Kitchen-goods specialist — turned wooden bowls and cutting boards as the entire catalog, with a sideline of trays and serving pieces. Single-piece live-edge cutting boards (no glue-up), turned bowls made from local cherry, walnut, and maple. Retail store on Route 4 between Quechee and Woodstock, full DTC site, wholesale. Pricing $90–$400 for boards, $50–$300 for bowls. Anchors the kitchen-goods-only positioning: a focused catalog can carry a brand without ever making furniture.

**Steven Noggle (turned vessels).** Morganton, North Carolina. Solo turner specializing in hollow-form vessels and bowls. Spalted maple, walnut, burls, ambrosia maple. Pieces sold as art objects rather than household goods — gallery representation, occasional museum shows. Eggshell-thin wall thicknesses (3/8 to 1/2 inch) achieved by turning the exterior, drying the blank, then hollowing through a narrow opening. Pricing tiered by size and rarity of the blank, from $200 small pieces to $2,000+ for exhibition vessels. Represents the woodturning-as-fine-art positioning that's often mistaken for kitchen-bowl turning.

**Sallie Plumley Studio.** Richmond, Virginia. One-woman custom-commission shop. Hand-cut joinery (mortise-and-tenon, dovetails, breadboard ends), built-to-spec furniture and small built-ins. Every project a one-off, designed around the client's space and use. Strong client-collaboration framing on the site — the maker walks the customer through design, wood selection, and finish choices. Mid-to-high pricing depending on scope. Anchors the modern solo-commission positioning: the woodworker who never builds the same piece twice.

**Offerman Woodshop.** Nick Offerman's shop in Los Angeles. Small crew of skilled woodworkers building made-to-order furniture, ukuleles, cutting boards, mustache combs, and the occasional canoe. Built around a clear founder voice (Offerman's writing and YouTube presence) and a slow-craft ethic. Sells through their own site with long lead times (some pieces backordered for months). Pricing varies wildly across the catalog. Shows what a strong narrative shop voice does for a small woodworking team.

**McLaughlin Woodworks.** Baltimore, Maryland. Bespoke furniture and built-ins for clients across the mid-Atlantic. Patchworked design details (geometric inlay, contrast-grain panels) as a signature. Mix of design-build commissions and standalone pieces. Anchors the regional design-build positioning that overlaps with cabinetmakers and millworkers.

The personalized-gift sub-segment on Etsy is its own positioning — shops running a laser engraver or CNC to produce wood signs, engraved cutting boards, family-name plaques, wedding gifts, and shelf-sitters at scale. WoodSignStudio (9,000+ favorites on a single listing), BoardSmith, and similar shops live here. Many of these shops describe themselves as woodworkers and would be Bohdi's customer; they're a real positioning, not a footnote.

A tenant could land anywhere across the range above, or in a positioning that isn't on the list — a restoration-only shop, a tiki-bar maker, a wooden-toy maker, an instrument luthier, a sign carver, an architectural-salvage repurposer. The category is wide; the brand exemplars are anchors, not the whole map.

## Who their customers are

**Furniture buyers** purchasing a heirloom-tier piece for a home: a dining table, bed frame, dresser, set of chairs. These buyers are usually pre-sold on solid wood and looking for confirmation on species, joinery, and the maker's track record. They compare across two or three shops, ask about turnaround, and often visit the shop or attend a delivery.

**Kitchen-and-table-goods buyers** picking up a cutting board, salad bowl, or serving piece. Self-purchase and gift purchase are both heavy here. These buyers respond to wood species (walnut and cherry outperform poplar and oak in this market), edge style (live edge vs. square), and food-safety vocabulary. Mid-tier pricing, repeat purchases on consumables (replacements, gifts).

**Custom-commission clients** with a specific brief — a built-in bookshelf around a window, a desk for an awkward room, a sideboard to match an existing piece, a vanity for a specific bathroom. These clients are willing to wait and pay more, and the maker's portfolio and process language close the sale. Often referred by an interior designer or architect.

**Designers, architects, and contractors** sourcing built-ins, millwork, cabinetry, custom doors, mantels. Less interested in narrative and more interested in lead time, CAD compatibility, install logistics, and how the shop handles a punch list.

**Gift buyers** looking for a personalized wood sign, engraved cutting board, monogram piece, baby-name shelf-sitter, or wedding plaque. The personalization step is the entire purchase — make-or-break copy is what shows up engraved. Price sensitive ($25–$120), volume-driven, fast turnaround expected.

**Collectors and gallery buyers** purchasing turned vessels, sculptural pieces, art-furniture. These buyers care about provenance, the maker's biography, blank rarity, and exhibition history. Pricing untethered from utility — value is in the artist's reputation and the singular piece.

**Restoration clients** with a piece they want repaired, refinished, or stripped and re-stained. A grandmother's dresser, a found-at-the-curb chair, a water-damaged tabletop. The clarity of the shop's repair process, before/after photos, and pricing transparency all matter here.

Recurring concerns across most segments: whether the piece will warp, crack, or move with seasonal humidity changes; how to care for the finish (re-oiling, conditioning, food-safety); whether wood from photos will match the actual piece; whether a custom dimension is possible; lead time on made-to-order; shipping logistics for large pieces (crating, freight, white-glove); whether the shop can match an existing piece in species and finish; whether the wood is food-safe; and whether the wood is locally or sustainably sourced.

Price ranges vary as widely as the catalog. A turned salad bowl runs $60–$250. A solid-wood cutting board runs $75–$400. A small side table runs $400–$1,200. A dining table runs $2,000–$15,000+. A custom built-in runs by linear foot, typically $400–$1,200/ft installed. Sculptural turned vessels and gallery-tier furniture run by the piece, often above $1,500 and into five figures.

## How they talk about their products

The vocabulary is dominated by wood species names, joinery method, and finish. Every product description that earns the buyer's attention names the wood: black walnut, American cherry, white oak, red oak, hard maple, soft maple, ash, hickory, sycamore, butternut, padauk, mesquite, sapele, mahogany, teak, koa. Figured stock gets called out specifically — curly maple, birdseye maple, quilted maple, spalted maple, fiddleback, burl, crotch. The buyer learns these words from the maker, not the other way around.

Joinery vocabulary signals craft seriousness: mortise-and-tenon (the structural joint of solid-wood furniture), dovetail (the visible joint of fine casework), wedged through-tenon (visible-from-the-top reinforcement), breadboard ends (cross-grain end cap on a table top that allows for wood movement), butterfly key (decorative-and-functional inlay across a crack), finger joint, half-lap, miter, scarf joint, biscuit, domino. Hand-cut joinery (chisels and saw) and machine-cut joinery (router, jig, CNC) both have a place; the maker's positioning shows in which they emphasize.

Finish vocabulary: oil finish (mineral oil, walnut oil, tung oil), hard wax oil (Osmo, Rubio Monocoat), Danish oil, shellac (often called out as French polish at the high end), beeswax, lacquer, conversion varnish, polyurethane, milk paint, soap finish, raw and unfinished. Food-safe vs. furniture-safe is a binary buyers ask about for kitchen goods. The maker who lists exact products (Mahoney's walnut oil, Tried & True original) reads more credible than the one who just writes "oil finish."

Construction vocabulary: solid wood (no plywood, no MDF), edge-grain, end-grain (cutting boards, butcher blocks), face-grain, slab (a single board the whole width of the piece), book-matched (two slabs from the same log opened like a book), live edge (the natural edge of the log left intact, sometimes filled with epoxy), through-tenon, frame-and-panel, breadboard, cradle, finger-joint, dovetail, kiln-dried, air-dried.

Naming strategies vary. Heritage shops often use proper names with species attached ("The Cumberland in Cherry," "The Edgecomb Side Table"). Modern design-led shops give pieces short conceptual names ("Slab Bench," "Field Stool," "Object 02"). Kitchen-goods specialists name by form and dimension ("12-inch Live Edge Cutting Board," "Salad Bowl, Walnut"). Turned-vessel artists name by form, blank, and serial ("Hollow Form #34, Spalted Maple"). Personalized-gift shops name by use ("Wedding Last Name Sign, Custom").

The maker's story carries real weight here in a way it doesn't in every craft category. Buyers want to know who shaped the piece, where the wood came from, how long the shop has been running. A two-paragraph about page with a real face and a real workshop wins against a hundred SKUs without one. The story doesn't have to be heritage — a first-generation maker who started in their garage four years ago has a story that lands.

## Common specializations and variations

**Product category.** Tables (dining, coffee, side, console, work), seating (chairs, benches, stools), case goods (dressers, sideboards, bookshelves), beds and bedroom, kitchen-and-table goods (cutting boards, bowls, spoons, serving pieces, trays), turned vessels and sculpture, smaller objects (boxes, frames, candleholders), instruments, built-ins and architectural millwork, restoration, signage and engraving.

**Wood species.** Black walnut, American cherry, white oak, red oak, hard maple, ash, hickory, sycamore, butternut, mahogany, sapele, teak, padauk, koa, mesquite, salvaged or reclaimed lumber, locally-sourced stock. Figured stock — curly, birdseye, quilted, spalted, burl, crotch — sold at a premium.

**Construction method.** Solid wood (no plywood), traditional joinery (mortise-and-tenon, dovetails, draw-bored, wedged), hybrid traditional plus modern, CNC-assisted, frame-and-panel, slab, plank-and-batten, turned, carved, steam-bent, bent-laminate.

**Finish.** Oil finish (mineral, walnut, tung), hard wax oil (Osmo, Rubio Monocoat), shellac (including French polish), Danish oil, beeswax, lacquer, conversion varnish, polyurethane, milk paint, ebonized, fumed (ammonia-darkened oak), raw, food-safe vs. furniture-safe.

**Edge style.** Square, eased, beveled, chamfered, roundover, ogee, live edge, waney edge.

**Personalization.** Engraving (laser, hand-carved, CNC), monogram, hand-stamped, branded, painted lettering, inlay name plates.

**Custom and bespoke service.** Made-to-order with specific dimensions, choice of species, choice of finish, scribe-to-fit installs, design-build collaborations with the client, matching to existing pieces.

**Repair and restoration.** Refinishing, re-gluing, replacement parts (turned legs, spindles, pulls), strip-and-stain, leveling, hardware refit, structural repair.

## What customers ask before buying

- What species is this made from, and how will it look once it ages?
- Is this solid wood throughout, or are there veneer panels?
- How is it joined? Will the joinery hold over time?
- What finish is on it, and is it food-safe?
- How do I care for it — re-oiling, conditioning, indoor humidity?
- Will the wood move, warp, or crack with the seasons?
- Can I get this in a different size, species, or finish?
- What's the lead time on a custom order or commission?
- How does shipping work for a piece this size? Is it crated? White-glove?
- Will the wood match my existing furniture? Can I see a sample?
- Can you match a specific piece I already have?

## Adjacent niches

Woodworkers commonly overlap with frame makers (matting and gallery framing in shared shop conditions), upholsterers (chairs and seating cushions sourced together), furniture restorers (some shops do both new builds and restoration), sculptors (turned-vessel artists especially overlap with the sculpture world), and cabinetmakers/millworkers (full-shop overlap on built-ins and architectural work). Toy makers, sign makers, instrument builders, and bookbinders share the woodworking material vocabulary and tooling. The line between "woodworker" and these adjacent crafts is regional and personal — many shops do two or three of them under one roof.
