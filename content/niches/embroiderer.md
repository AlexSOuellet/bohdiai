# Embroiderer & Needlepoint

## What this business does

An embroiderer stitches thread into cloth to make images, lettering, and decoration. In practice this covers three distinct crafts that share a vocabulary but not a workflow. **Hand embroidery** is free-stitched by hand on woven fabric — hoop art, framed pieces, embroidered patches, stitched apparel, and thread-painted portraits. **Needlepoint** (and its cousin counted cross-stitch) works stitches over the holes of a stiff canvas or an even-count fabric, following a printed canvas or a charted pattern — finished pillows, Christmas stockings, belts, key fobs, and framed samplers. **Machine embroidery** runs a digitized design through a single-head or multi-head machine to stitch logos, monograms, and lettering onto hats, polos, tote bags, and workwear, usually as a service for other businesses.

The output splits into finished goods, made-to-order commissions, and materials for other stitchers. Finished goods are the hoop art, the framed piece, the completed pillow or stocking, the embroidered garment. Commissions are custom work — a pet portrait from a photo, a wedding hoop with a couple's vows, a company's logo digitized and run onto a batch of uniforms. Materials are the other half of the market entirely: hand-painted needlepoint canvases sold blank for the buyer to stitch, DIY kits packed with pre-printed fabric and floss, PDF and charted patterns, stick-and-stitch designs, and stitch guides. Many makers sell across all three — a finished-goods line, a custom-order queue, and a shelf of kits and patterns that keep revenue moving between commissions.

Distribution runs through their own online shop, Etsy, needlework trade shows and markets, wholesale to local yarn and needlepoint stores, and — for the machine-embroidery and monogramming shops — direct B2B contracts with teams, schools, and small businesses. Turnaround varies enormously: a stick-and-stitch pattern ships same week, a digitized logo comes back in 24 hours, and a hand-stitched thread-painted portrait can take six to eight weeks from the moment the maker has usable reference photos.

## Brand exemplars across the range

**Stitching Sabbatical (Michelle Staub).** An Ohio hand-embroidery artist who built her name on custom pet portraits done in thread painting — "immortalize your pet with a thread painting," memorial work included. The business is a stack: one-of-a-kind commissions, a published book (*Pet Portrait Embroidery*), an online course, mentorships, a free starter pattern, and a shop of patterns, kits, pins, and stickers. The voice is warm, personal, and teacher-forward; the artist and her story are the brand.

**Morgan Julia Designs.** A Jersey City studio designing and hand-painting needlepoint canvases with a classic, preppy, nautical bent "designed with a modern twist." Canvases run roughly $30 to $285, organized by category (Americana, Bar Cart, Travel, Holiday) and by stitch level 1 through 3, offered in 13-count and 18-count mesh, sold alongside thread, stitch guides, and beginner bundles. Positioned as an accessible on-ramp for new stitchers as much as a canvas line for the experienced.

**Haley Hamilton Art.** A DIY-kit and pattern shop built for beginners — botanical hand-embroidery kits, stick-and-stitch design packs, PDF patterns, and supplies, most priced $12 to $36. Everything comes with step-by-step instructions and video tutorials; the review wall is full of "my first time trying embroidery and I loved it." The positioning is teaching-first and gift-friendly, selling the confidence to start as much as the materials.

**Badass Cross Stitch (Shannon Downey) and Subversive / Feminist Cross-Stitch (Stephanie Rohr).** The craftivist wing — needlework as gentle protest. Downey calls herself an "artist, activist, craftivist, community builder, and general instigator"; Rohr's *Feminist Cross-Stitch* organizes patterns into chapters like "Stitched Affirmations" and "Politically Active." The voice is witty, pointed, and unapologetic, taking a craft coded as domestic and turning it into commentary. Sells patterns, kits, and finished pieces to a buyer who wants the message as much as the stitching.

**Lycette Designs and Rittenhouse Needlepoint.** The heirloom end of needlepoint — hand-painted Christmas stocking canvases and finished pieces framed explicitly as "heirlooms," painted on quality Zwigart canvas, often sold with finishing and stitch-guide services so the buyer receives a completed keepsake rather than a project. Positioning leans on tradition, permanence, and the idea of an object handed down.

**Local monogramming and machine-embroidery shops.** The B2B service tier — a shop that digitizes a logo and runs it onto caps, polos, aprons, and workwear for teams, restaurants, and small businesses, plus retail monogramming of towels, robes, and baby gifts. Pricing is transactional: digitizing a logo runs roughly $10 to $50 depending on size and stitch count, with per-piece decoration on top and 24-hour turnaround as a selling point. The voice is service-oriented and reliability-focused — speed, consistency, and getting the brand mark right.

**TheHoopInHand and the modern hoop-art / pattern makers.** Contemporary designers selling "timeless hand embroidery patterns for modern makers" — seasonal hoop art, felt decorations, and patterns aimed at the maker who wants a fresh, current design rather than a traditional sampler. Positioning is design-led and community-facing, often built on an Instagram or YouTube following.

A tenant in this niche could land anywhere across this range — and in places not listed here, including sashiko and visible-mending makers, ecclesiastical and goldwork specialists, historical-sampler reproducers, embroidered-jewelry makers, and custom wedding-and-event stitchers. The exemplars are anchors for the breadth of the category, not a menu to pick from.

## Who their customers are

**Self-buyers and makers** buy for themselves — the finished hoop for their own wall, or (just as often) the kit, canvas, or pattern to stitch themselves. This second group is enormous in this niche: the customer isn't buying an object, they're buying a project. They care about skill level, clear instructions, and whether they'll actually be able to finish it.

**Gift buyers** choose embroidery for milestone moments — a stitched birth announcement, a wedding hoop, a monogrammed towel set, a needlepoint stocking that becomes a family fixture. They value personalization and want clear guidance on what reads as the right choice.

**Memorial and commission clients** come with a photo and a story — a pet that has passed, a portrait, a piece of handwriting to preserve in thread. They're buying meaning, they'll wait weeks, and they need to trust the maker's track record before they commit.

**Business and team buyers** are the machine-embroidery customer — a restaurant that needs aprons, a league that needs caps, a startup putting its logo on staff polos. They care about digitizing quality, color matching, turnaround, and minimums.

**Collectors and traditional stitchers** buy hand-painted needlepoint canvases and heirloom pieces as an established, sometimes expensive hobby. They know their mesh counts, they have opinions about fibers, and they'll follow a favorite canvas designer's new releases.

**Subcultural and message buyers** want the craftivist piece, the pointed pattern, the stitched statement — the meaning carries the purchase.

Recurring concerns cut across segments: for kits and canvases, what skill level is honest and what exactly is included; for finished apparel and patches, whether the piece is washable and colorfast; for framed and hoop pieces, how it arrives finished and ready to hang; for commissions, turnaround time and how many revisions; and for monogramming, whether initials, fonts, and thread colors can be specified. Price ranges span the widest of any fiber niche: PDF patterns and stick-and-stitch packs run $6 to $15; DIY kits $20 to $45; hand-painted needlepoint canvases $30 to $300 depending on size and mesh; finished hoop art $40 to $150; custom thread-painted portraits $150 to $500+; and machine digitizing $10 to $50 per logo plus per-piece decoration. Positioning moves all of these.

## How they talk about their products

The vocabulary comes straight from the materials and the stitches. **Fiber:** stranded cotton floss (DMC is the reference brand, cited by color number — DMC 321, DMC 725), perle cotton, silk, wool, metallic thread; a skein of six-strand floss that stitchers separate to control thickness. **Ground fabric:** aida (the beginner-standard cotton with a visible block grid, sold by count — 14-count is the common one), evenweave and linen (finer, stitched "over two" threads, where 28-count evenweave gives the same size as 14-count aida), calico and muslin for free embroidery, and needlepoint canvas measured in mesh count (13-count and 18-count are the common pair, 13 more forgiving, 18 finer). Count is the single most-repeated spec in the category and buyers understand it.

**Stitches** signal both technique and skill: satin stitch, back stitch, chain stitch, stem stitch, the French knot (the stitch beginners fear), and long-and-short stitch — the shading stitch that makes thread painting read like a painted image. Needlepoint copy references basketweave, continental, and decorative stitches, often bundled into a **stitch guide** that tells the buyer which stitch goes where. **Machine** vocabulary is its own dialect: **digitizing** (converting artwork into a stitch file), stitch count, 3D puff, appliqué, and **stabilizer** — tear-away, cut-away, and wash-away backings, plus water-soluble **waste canvas** and **stick-and-stitch** designs that let a stitcher work a counted or printed design onto ordinary clothing and then dissolve the guide away.

Naming strategies split by tier. Pattern and canvas makers name descriptively and playfully — "Dirty Martini," "Hot Dog Cart," "Ski Slope Soiree" from a preppy needlepoint line; "Kawaii Stick & Stitch" and "Bee Kind" from a beginner kit shop. Craftivist makers name for the message. Commission artists name for the subject and the sentiment — the pet's name, "memorial," "immortalize." What reads flat is generic craft-supply language: "beautiful embroidery kit," "high-quality thread," "great for all skill levels." Specifics carry the copy — the count, the stitch, the fiber, the finished dimensions, the honest skill level. The maker's story does heavy lifting at the commission and craftivist ends, where buyers are choosing a person as much as a product.

## Common specializations and variations

**Craft type.** Hand embroidery, counted cross-stitch, needlepoint, machine embroidery and monogramming, thread painting, sashiko and visible mending, goldwork and metal-thread, crewel wool embroidery.

**Product format.** Finished hoop art, framed pieces, embroidered patches, stitched apparel, finished needlepoint pillows and stockings, DIY kits, PDF and charted patterns, hand-painted needlepoint canvases (sold blank), stick-and-stitch designs, stitch guides.

**Subject and style.** Botanical and floral, pet and human portraits, lettering and quotes, monograms, preppy and nautical, Americana and folk, seasonal and holiday, modern and abstract, craftivist and message-driven.

**Fabric and count.** Aida (14, 16, 18-count), evenweave and linen (28, 32-count), needlepoint mesh (13-count, 18-count), calico and muslin, garment and apparel grounds.

**Fiber.** Stranded cotton (DMC and similar), perle cotton, silk, wool, metallic and specialty threads, hand-dyed floss.

**Finishing.** Hooped and mounted, framed, made into pillows or ornaments, backed with felt, patch-finished with iron-on or sew-on backing, professional needlepoint finishing service.

**Skill level.** Beginner kits with video tutorials, intermediate patterns, advanced thread painting and decorative-stitch canvases — often labeled with an explicit level so the buyer chooses honestly.

**Personalization and service.** Monograms and names, custom colors and fonts, portraits from photos, logo digitizing, wedding and event pieces, revisions and proofs.

Treat these as starting points a maker can mix and rename, not a fixed set of categories.

## What customers ask before buying

The AI's job in product descriptions, FAQ, and storefront copy is to answer these before the customer has to ask:

- Is this a finished piece, a kit, a blank canvas, or a pattern — and what exactly comes in the box?
- What skill level is this honestly for, and are instructions or video tutorials included?
- What fabric count or mesh count is it, and what thread does it use?
- If it's apparel or a patch, is it washable and colorfast, and how do I care for it?
- Does the hoop or framed piece arrive ready to hang?
- For a custom portrait or commission, how many photos do you need, how long does it take, and how many revisions do I get?
- Can I get initials, a monogram, or custom colors and fonts?
- For monogramming and logos, do you digitize the artwork, what are the minimums, and what's the turnaround?
- What size is the finished piece?
- What's the return policy on custom and personalized work?

## Adjacent niches

Embroiderers commonly overlap with knitting and crochet, quilting, and general fiber and textile art — many makers stitch across several. The pattern-and-kit side shares a business model with any craft that sells materials and instruction, and the machine-embroidery and monogramming side overlaps with screen printing, custom apparel, and promotional-products shops. Embroidered jewelry, brooches, and accessories bridge into the jewelry and accessories categories. The material vocabulary here — floss, count, fabric type, stitch names, stabilizer, digitizing — carries directly into those adjacent files.
