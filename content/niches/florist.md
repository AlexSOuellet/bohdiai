# Florist

## What this business does

A florist designs, arranges, and sells flowers. The operational range is wide: some florists run a full brick-and-mortar shop with daily walk-in sales; others work as studio florists operating by appointment only from a home or rented space, primarily serving weddings and events; others run a flower farm and sell at farmers markets, in subscriptions, or direct to other florists. Many sit somewhere between those points — a small studio maker who does custom arrangements, weekly subscriptions, and the occasional wedding.

The work involves sourcing stems (from local farms, wholesale flower markets, or growers networks like Slow Flowers), conditioning them, and designing arrangements to order or for standing inventory. Local, seasonal, and farm-direct sourcing is a growing differentiator in the category, with "farm-to-vase" positioned the way "farm-to-table" is in food. Flowers are highly perishable, which shapes the entire model: subscription and standing order customers are valuable because they smooth demand and reduce waste.

Distribution channels include in-store walk-in sales, local delivery, wedding and event contracts, weekly or monthly subscription boxes, workshops and floral design classes, and direct-to-consumer shipping for dried or preserved arrangements.

## Brand exemplars across the range

**Farmgirl Flowers (direct-to-consumer, sustainability-forward, farm-connected).** Founded in San Francisco by Christina Stembel, built around simple burlap-wrapped bouquets sourced from domestic farms. Voice is direct and founder-visible. Strong "American-grown" and anti-waste positioning. A rare example of a florist that scaled direct-to-consumer nationally.

**Lewis Miller Design (luxury event, editorial-forward, cultural spectacle).** Known for "flower flash" guerrilla installations and major event work in New York. Positioned at the very high end of event florals. Website is portfolio-heavy, press-credentialed. The brand sells the designer's vision, not a product line.

**Putnam & Putnam (luxury editorial, moody, painterly).** New York-based studio known for rich jewel-tone palette, overflowing compositions, and editorial collaborations. Primarily event and wedding clients. Their imagery reads more like oil paintings than arrangements.

**Flowerbx (curated stems, single-variety, minimal).** London-origin DTC brand built around single-variety bunches shipped in boxes. The aesthetic is pared-down and intentional. A different philosophy than mixed-arrangement brands — championing the beauty of one stem type rather than the abundance of many.

**Local farmers market flower farmer (seasonal, community-connected, lower-priced).** A grower-arranger who sells at the stand on Saturdays, offers CSA flower shares, and posts growing updates on Instagram. The brand is the farmer's face and the farm's story. Voice is warm, unpolished, and personal. Product moves fast at $12–$35 a bunch.

**Dried and preserved floral brands (Etsy-native, minimal maintenance, year-round gifting).** Pampas grass, strawflowers, billy buttons, cotton stems, eucalyptus. The value proposition is longevity — arrangements that last months or years. Often positioned in boho or Scandi-minimal directions. Often DTC via Etsy or their own site.

**Subscription-first florists (recurring, local, relationship-led).** A florist who has built their business around weekly or monthly subscriptions rather than one-off sales. Voice emphasizes the ritual of fresh flowers, the seasonal story, the relationship between maker and recipient. Sells scarcity and anticipation as much as flowers.

A tenant could land anywhere across this range, including places not on this list — the mood pick and inspiration URLs are what choose direction.

## Who their customers are

Customers in this category span several distinct motivations.

**Gift buyers** are a major segment in every direction. Birthday, anniversary, sympathy, housewarming, congratulations, and "thinking of you" occasions drive a substantial share of orders. Gift buyers often don't know exactly what they want — they want it to look right for the occasion and the person. Best-seller recommendations and curated occasion collections do real work here.

**Self-buyers** who keep fresh flowers as part of their home environment. These customers often become subscription customers — they buy repeatedly, they notice the seasons changing in their arrangements, and they develop loyalty to a specific maker's aesthetic.

**Wedding and event clients** looking for a florist to design ceremony flowers, reception centerpieces, ceremony arches, bouquets, boutonnieres, and installation florals. This is a high-value, high-trust transaction that requires a portfolio, a clear style point of view, and a consultation process.

**Interior and commercial clients** — restaurants, offices, hotels, real estate staging — who want standing weekly orders or one-off installs. Usually lower-margin but higher-volume and more predictable.

**Workshop students** drawn to a florist's aesthetic who want to learn the craft. Workshops have become a real revenue stream for many florists and also function as brand-building.

Recurring customer concerns: whether flowers will arrive fresh and last; how long an arrangement will last if cared for; what to do with dying flowers; whether the florist can match a specific style or color palette; and for weddings, whether the florist has experience at specific venues.

Price ranges vary considerably. A farmers market mixed bunch might be $12–$35. A standard delivery arrangement runs $45–$150. Wedding packages commonly range from $1,500 to $15,000+ depending on scale and market. High-end event installations are priced by quote.

## How they talk about their products

Florists build their vocabulary around the season, the source, the occasion, and the feeling. Common product language includes specific flower names rather than generic category names — "garden roses," "ranunculus," "fritillaria," "scabiosa," "foxglove," "Icelandic poppies" — because specificity signals expertise and helps customers picture what they're getting.

Color palette vocabulary tends to be painterly and emotional: "dusty mauve and champagne," "burnt coral and chocolate cosmos," "jewel tones spilling into burgundy." Florists who use decorator-swatch language ("sage green, blush") often feel less expert than those who use botanical and sensory language.

Naming strategies vary by positioning. Event-focused florists often name products by the occasion or sentiment ("The Sympathy Collection," "The Morning Bride"). Farm florists often name by the flower or the season. Subscription florists often describe what's blooming now and why it matters.

The maker's relationship to growing, sourcing, and seasonality is a differentiator worth writing into product copy. "This week's arrangement includes foxglove from our farm, coral charm peonies from a grower in Oregon, and sweet William we've been waiting all spring for" is a real selling statement in this category.

Generic adjectives — "gorgeous," "stunning," "beautiful" — read flat because every florist uses them. Specific botanical language and seasonal specificity do the work instead.

## Common specializations and variations

**Arrangement style.** Garden style (lush, overflowing, romantic); structural/architectural; ikebana-influenced (spare, intentional); wild/foraged (loose stems, textural, meadow-like); minimalist single-variety.

**Flower source.** Locally grown, domestic farm-direct, wholesale/imported, foraged. Slow Flowers is a movement/network that florists cite as a sourcing differentiator.

**Longevity.** Fresh cut, dried, preserved (glycerin-treated), freeze-dried. Dried and preserved arrangements have different marketing logic — longevity is the headline.

**Occasion.** Everyday gifting, weddings, events, sympathy, corporate, holidays. Many florists specialize in one.

**Product type.** Bouquets, arrangements in vessels, single stems, wreaths, garlands, flower crowns, buttonholes/boutonnieres, ceremony arches, table runners, installations.

**Subscription patterns.** Weekly, biweekly, monthly. Seasonal subscriptions. CSA flower shares (inspired by farm CSA models). Gift subscriptions.

**Workshops.** Beginner arrangement workshops, seasonal wreath-making, dried flower pressing, flower farm tours. Often sold as events or experiences rather than products.

## What customers ask before buying

- How long will the flowers last, and what care do they need?
- Can I specify colors, flowers, or a vibe, or is the arrangement maker's choice?
- Is same-day or next-day delivery available?
- Do you deliver to my area?
- Can I see more examples of your style before ordering?
- For weddings: Do you have experience at my venue? Can we do a consultation?
- For subscriptions: How does the subscription work — am I locked in? How much notice to pause or cancel?
- For dried arrangements: Do these need any care? Will they fade over time?

Storefronts that answer these clearly tend to outperform ones that don't.

## Visual direction range

This category contains a wide range of visual sensibilities — not a single dominant aesthetic — and the mood pick and inspiration URLs are what choose among them.

- **Lush garden romantic:** Overflowing compositions, muted blush and dusty mauve and champagne palettes, serif type, soft natural-light photography. Common in wedding-focused and gift-focused directions.
- **Painterly jewel-tone luxury:** Deep burgundy, oxblood, aubergine, and terracotta compositions against neutral or moody backgrounds. Feels editorial, like an oil painting. Common in high-end event and editorial positioning.
- **Minimal single-variety:** Clean white or soft-neutral backgrounds, one flower type per image, architectural composition. Common in DTC brands emphasizing quality over abundance.
- **Farm and field wild:** Loose, textural, outdoors-shot arrangements. Sunlight, imperfection, seasonal specificity. Common in flower farm and farmers market positioning.
- **Dried and earthy:** Pampas, neutral straw tones, terracotta vessels, textured plaster backgrounds. Common in boho and Scandi-minimal dried floral directions.
- **Dark and moody botanical:** Black backgrounds, dramatic side-lighting, foliage-heavy compositions. Common in luxury and art-forward positioning.
- **Clean modern studio:** Bright, even light, white backgrounds, tight crops. More commercial-feeling, often used in DTC and corporate floristry.

What holds true across all visual directions: close-up photography of individual stems and flower faces carries tremendous storefront value. The texture of a ranunculus petal, the curl of a sweetpea tendril, the weight of a fully open peony — these shots do the emotional work regardless of which aesthetic direction the storefront takes.

## What tends to surface on the storefront

The mood pick and inspiration URLs drive the layout — this section just lists what tends to be useful.

**A featured arrangement or seasonal collection.** Florists live by the season. A rotating hero or featured collection that names what's blooming right now does real work in showing the maker's active relationship with their material.

**An occasion or use filter.** Buyers often arrive with an occasion in mind (birthday, sympathy, wedding, treat myself). Organizing by occasion alongside style or flower type helps gift-buyers navigate.

**A gallery or portfolio section.** For event and wedding florists, the portfolio is the primary selling tool. Large photography, categorized by event type, anchors the service.

**A subscription or CSA section.** For florists with recurring revenue models, the subscription deserves its own block or page — including what to expect, how often, how to pause.

**A workshop events block.** If the florist runs workshops, these are worth a visible section — they're both a revenue stream and a brand-building touch that shows the depth of expertise.

**A sourcing and story section.** In this category, who grows the flowers and how matters to a real share of buyers. Slow Flowers, local farms, seasonal specificity — worth surfacing in an about or sourcing section.

**A care guide block or FAQ.** Questions about flower longevity and care recur across every segment. A short, warm care block reduces post-purchase anxiety and sets expectations.

## What to avoid

- **Stock flower photography.** The florist's own arrangements are the product. Generic stock images undercut the maker's specific style, which is exactly what buyers are choosing.
- **Vague mood language without specific flowers.** "Beautiful blooms for every occasion" reads like a copy template. "Foxglove, coral charm peonies, and sweet William" reads like a maker.
- **Hiding the seasonal reality.** Florists who pretend every flower is always available erode trust. Seasonal specificity is a strength, not a limitation.
- **Burying the maker's story.** The person behind the arrangements matters in this category — their aesthetic, their sourcing relationships, their seasonal sensibility. Storefronts that lead with products and skip the maker tend to feel interchangeable.
- **Generic occasion copy.** "The perfect gift for birthdays, anniversaries, and more" is in every flower shop. Specific seasonal or stylistic language differentiates.
- **Ignoring longevity information.** Customers consistently ask how long arrangements last and what to do to extend them. Not answering this is a missed opportunity at every price point.

## Adjacent niches

Florists commonly expand into dried floral and wreath making, botanical prints and art, floral workshops, flower farming, event styling and decor, and candle or fragrance products using florals (rose, lavender, jasmine). Wedding florists sometimes broaden into full event design or coordination. Farmers-market florists sometimes add herb bundles, dried botanicals, or herbal products to their stand. Writers of adjacent files for wedding_planner, event_planner, and herbalist should note the crossover customer: someone who loves flowers and seasonality often also loves botanical wellness products and seasonal home goods. The florist's vocabulary (botanical specificity, seasonal grounding, maker visibility) translates directly into those adjacent niches.
