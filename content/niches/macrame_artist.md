# Macramé Artist

## What this business does

A macramé artist knots cord by hand into decorative and functional textiles. The range is wide: wall hangings, plant hangers, table runners and placemats, mirror surrounds, garlands and bunting, curtains and room dividers, market bags and purses, cushion covers, keychains, and jewelry. No sewing machine and no loom — the whole discipline is built from a small set of knots (square knot, half-hitch, lark's head, clove hitch, and a handful of decorative ones like the berry and Josephine) repeated and combined into pattern. The cord itself does most of the aesthetic work, so material choice is central: single-twist cotton string that combs out into soft fringe, 3-ply twisted rope that holds a crisp knot, braided cord that resists fraying, plus jute, hemp, wool roving, and recycled cotton. Cord weight is quoted in millimeters — roughly 2mm for jewelry and micro work up to 5 or 6mm for statement pieces.

Operations run from a maker at a dowel rack mounted on the wall, measuring and cutting long lengths of cord (a large hanging can eat well over a kilometer of string), to small studios producing a repeatable product line, to fine artists doing commissioned installations for hotels, restaurants, and corporate lobbies. Many makers mount finished work on a wooden dowel, brass or copper rod, or a piece of driftwood, then comb and trim the fringe. Turnaround on a stock plant hanger is short; a large custom wall piece or an installation can take days of knotting and weeks on the calendar.

Distribution spans their own online shop, Etsy, craft fairs and markets, wholesale to boutiques and plant shops, event and wedding rentals, and — a large part of this category specifically — teaching. Kits, downloadable PDF patterns, in-person and online workshops, and books are a real revenue line, not a sideline, and some makers earn more from teaching the craft than from selling finished pieces.

## Brand exemplars across the range

**Windy Chien.** San Francisco fiber artist who came to knots through a year-long daily project tying a different knot every day, then built a practice around large commissioned installations — rope sculptures, room dividers, and suspended pieces for hospitality and corporate spaces. Positioned as an artist and designer, not a home-decor shop; work is commission-led and priced accordingly.

**MACRO MACRAMÉ.** Brooklyn studio specializing in large-scale, custom hand-knotted fiber for interiors — oversized wall hangings, room dividers, screen sculptures, and installations for homes and hospitality. Pricing is quote-on-request rather than listed, which itself signals the bespoke, architectural-scale positioning.

**Emily Katz / Modern Macramé.** Portland-based teacher, author, and materials seller who built a business around education and lifestyle as much as objects — sold-out workshops worldwide, a widely sold book of projects, a curated cord-and-supplies shop, and custom installations for brands. The authority-and-community model: the maker is the draw, and the products, classes, and materials all extend from her.

**Mkono.** High-volume home-decor brand selling affordable macramé plant hangers and hanging planters through the big marketplaces, indoor and outdoor, often with wood-bead detailing. Positioned on accessibility and price rather than one-of-a-kind craft — the mass-market end of the same category.

**47th & Main.** Wholesale home-goods brand that supplies boho macramé plant hangers and decor to independent retailers. Trade-facing rather than direct-to-consumer, proving the category isn't only solo makers — it includes brands built to stock other people's shelves.

**Wedding and event backdrop studios** (rental-and-custom outfits such as event stylists and boho rental shops). These are doer-side businesses: they build macramé arches, ceremony backdrops, and feature walls, then rent or sell them, often shipping a piece that slides onto a dowel for easy setup. The sale is a date on the calendar and a look for a room, not a shelf product.

**Malka Macramé.** Micro-macramé and jewelry-focused maker and supplier — brass chokers, statement earrings, and fine knotted pieces in waxed thread, plus supplies aimed at other jewelry makers. Shows the category runs all the way down to 1mm–2mm work and body-worn pieces.

**Kit and pattern makers** (shops like Make and Fable and countless Etsy PDF sellers). Businesses whose core product is the ability to make it yourself — beginner keychain and plant-hanger kits with pre-cut cord and a dowel, downloadable step-by-step patterns, and knot guides. The buyer wants the experience of knotting, not a finished object.

A tenant in this niche could land anywhere across this range — fine-art commissions, boho home decor, wholesale supply, event rentals, jewelry, or teaching and kits — including combinations and positions not listed here. Treat these as anchors, not targets.

## Who their customers are

**Home decorators and self-buyers** furnishing a room — a plant hanger for a sunny corner, a wall hanging above the bed or couch, a runner for the table. They're matching a piece to a specific spot and care a lot about scale and color against their wall.

**Gift buyers** picking something handmade and warm for a housewarming, wedding, or holiday. Plant hangers and small wall hangings are common gifts; this segment wants clear guidance on what size and style to choose.

**Brides, event planners, and photographers** who need a backdrop, arch, or ceremony feature for a specific date. They're on a deadline, often booking rentals or commissioning custom work months ahead, and they care about setup, transport, and how it photographs.

**Plant people** who buy hangers as an extension of their plant habit — matching cord color and drop length to particular pots and hanging spots, indoors and on covered porches.

**Learners and makers** who buy kits, PDF patterns, cord and supplies, and workshop seats because they want to knot it themselves. For this segment the maker's clarity as a teacher is the product.

**Interior designers, stagers, and hospitality buyers** commissioning larger or custom pieces for clients and spaces, who need to talk dimensions, color matching, and lead time.

Recurring concerns across segments: whether a piece is the right size and scale for the wall or space; whether custom colors or dimensions are possible; how cord holds up to sunlight, humidity, and outdoor use (cotton fades and weakens in strong sun); how much it weighs and what hardware anchors it safely; whether the fringe or cord sheds; and, for events, whether it can be delivered in time and how setup works.

Price ranges vary widely by positioning. Keychains and small jewelry run roughly $8–$30. Plant hangers run $20–$70. Small-to-medium wall hangings run $40–$150; statement and large custom hangings run $150–$600 and up, with room-scale installations quoted by the project into the thousands. Wedding backdrop rentals commonly run $150–$400 for the date, with custom-purchase pieces higher. Kits run $20–$50, downloadable PDF patterns $5–$15, and workshop seats $40–$120. Positioning moves all of these.

## How they talk about their products

The vocabulary is built from cord and knots. Cord language signals what the buyer is getting: single-twist (or single-strand) string that brushes out into soft, silky fringe; 3-ply twisted rope that keeps a crisp defined knot but won't comb out; braided cord that resists fraying; and the fibers themselves — 100% cotton, recycled cotton, natural jute, hemp, wool roving, linen. Weight is quoted in millimeters (2mm, 3mm, 4mm, 5mm), and buyers of larger work learn to read it.

Knot names appear in descriptions and especially in patterns and tutorials: square knot and half-square (which spirals), lark's head and reverse lark's head (for mounting cord to a dowel), half-hitch and double half-hitch (worked diagonally for chevrons and leaves), clove hitch, and decorative knots like the berry, the Josephine, and gathering knots. Structural terms include the sennit (a column of repeated knots), the fringe, and combing or brushing out (teasing single-twist cord into a soft fan). Mounting vocabulary matters too: wooden dowel, brass or copper rod, driftwood, a ring for plant hangers.

Naming strategies run the full range. Descriptive and size-led ("Large Fringe Wall Hanging, 36 inches"). Place- and nature-led ("Dune," "Sierra," "Coastline"). Intention-led ("The Nursery Hanging," "Ceremony Backdrop"). Character names for signature designs. Boho and celestial references (moon, sun, feather, wave) are common enough that leaning entirely on them reads generic — the pieces that stand out pair a warm name with concrete specifics on size, cord, and drop. The maker's story — how they came to knots, whether the cord is recycled, where the work is made — does real selling work in a category defined by handmade warmth.

## Common specializations and variations

**Product category.** Wall hangings, plant hangers, table runners and placemats, mirror surrounds, garlands and bunting, curtains and room dividers, bags and purses, cushion covers, keychains, jewelry, wedding and event backdrops, and Christmas or seasonal pieces.

**Cord type.** Single-twist string (combs into fringe), 3-ply twisted rope (crisp knots), braided cord (durable, low fray).

**Fiber.** Cotton, recycled cotton, jute, hemp, wool roving, linen, and synthetic blends for outdoor or budget lines.

**Cord weight.** Fine (2mm and under, jewelry and micro-macramé) through mid (3–4mm, most home decor) to heavy (5–6mm, statement and large-scale work).

**Mount and hardware.** Wooden dowel, brass or copper rod, driftwood, rings for plant hangers, and the anchoring hardware the buyer needs on their end.

**Scale.** Mini and desk-sized, standard home-decor sizes, and statement or room-scale installations.

**Color.** Natural undyed cotton, dyed cords, and custom color matching for commissions and events.

**Custom and commission service.** Made-to-order dimensions and colors, event and wedding pieces, hospitality and interior installations.

**Teaching and DIY.** Kits with pre-cut cord, downloadable PDF patterns, knot guides, in-person and online workshops, and books.

Treat these as starting suggestions a maker picks from, not a fixed schema.

## What customers ask before buying

The AI's job in product descriptions, FAQ, and storefront copy is to answer these without the customer having to ask:

- How big is it, and will that scale look right on my wall or in my space?
- Can I get it in a custom size, color, or design?
- What cord and fiber is it made from, and will the fringe shed?
- Can it go outside or in a bathroom, and will sunlight fade it?
- How much does it weigh, and what do I need to hang it safely?
- How is it mounted — dowel, rod, driftwood — and is the hardware included?
- How do I clean and care for it without ruining the knots?
- For events: can you deliver by my date, and do you rent or sell? How does setup work?
- For kits and patterns: what's included, what skill level is it, and are there video guides for the knots?

## Adjacent niches

Macramé artists commonly expand into weaving and tapestry (many sell both fiber wall art and woven pieces), knitting and crochet, punch needle, and other fiber crafts that share the handmade-textile buyer. Jewelry is a natural extension through micro-macramé, and home-decor and plant-adjacent lines (planters, pottery, dried florals) round out a boho storefront. Event-focused makers overlap with wedding and floral styling. The cord, knot, and fiber vocabulary here carries directly into those adjacent categories, and content overlap with the weaver, knitter, and jeweler niche files is expected.
